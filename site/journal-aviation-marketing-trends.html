Aviation marketing trends brokers can’t ignore

Five shifts we see in how business aircraft are marketed — what’s driving each one, and what single brokers and established teams can do about it, starting with the next listing.

The aircraft are as remarkable as ever. What has changed is the way buyers meet them. Discovery happens on phones, timelines are tighter, expectations of presentation keep rising, and the people writing the checks — or advising those who do — expect information to arrive quickly, completely and discreetly.

Below are five shifts we see shaping how business aircraft are marketed, with a practical response to each. None of them requires reinventing how you work. Most of them reward the brokers who adopt them early.

Vertical video moves to the front

Short vertical video has become one of the main ways people discover things on their phones, and aircraft are no exception. A twenty-second clip of an airstair unfolding at dusk, or a slow pan across a refurbished cabin, can reach brokers, buyers and their advisors in places a listing marketplace never will: on Instagram, and on LinkedIn, where so much of the industry’s professional conversation already happens.

The response: plan vertical at the shoot, not in the edit. Cropping a horizontal film into a 9:16 frame almost always loses the composition. Keep each clip to one idea — the cabin, the cockpit, a single standout upgrade — and put the key specifications on screen, because many people watch with the sound off. Then pace the clips across a listing’s launch instead of posting everything on day one.

Every serious aircraft gets its own address

Listing marketplaces remain essential, but they present every aircraft in the same template, right next to its competitors. A dedicated listing microsite — a single, well-designed page for one aircraft — gives the story room to breathe: the full gallery, the film, a downloadable spec sheet and a clear way to enquire.

Its real advantage is control. A microsite can be shared as one clean link in a message to a principal. It can be gated, so the full specification is only visible after an NDA or with a password. It can show the broker which sections people actually viewed, which makes the follow-up call far more useful. And when the aircraft sells, it can come down in seconds.

Inventory, connected by data

Few things undermine trust faster than inconsistency: a different total time on two platforms, a price updated in one place but not another, an aircraft still shown as available weeks after it sold. As listings spread across marketplaces, websites, social channels and CRMs, keeping them aligned by hand becomes a job in itself, and a reliable source of mistakes.

The answer we see taking hold is connected inventory: one source of truth for each aircraft’s specifications, imagery, price position and status, pushed to every channel through data feeds and API connections. Update it once and it updates everywhere. The same connections bring data back — which channels produce enquiries, which produce qualified ones, and where the marketing budget is actually working.

Faster launches don’t come from rushing the creative. They come from removing the waiting.

Launches in days, not weeks

Sellers have little patience for a long gap between signing a listing agreement and seeing their aircraft on the market, and with reason. Every week spent assembling assets is a week the aircraft isn’t being seen.

Faster launches don’t come from rushing the creative. They come from removing the waiting. Spec sheet and microsite templates ready before the shoot. Shot lists agreed in advance. Photography, film and design running in parallel rather than one after another. Clear approval steps with the seller, so nothing sits unanswered in an inbox. Run this way, a listing can go live in days with nothing left unfinished.

Discretion becomes a feature

Many sellers of business aircraft value privacy as much as price. Some don’t want the aircraft identifiable at all. Others are content to be seen, but not to have their travel, their home base or their name attached to the listing. Marketing now has to be designed around that from the first day, not patched afterwards.

Privacy-conscious marketing takes several forms: registrations masked in imagery when the seller asks; neutral locations rather than a recognizable home base; cabins photographed without personal items; full specifications released only to verified buyers or under NDA; targeted outreach to a short list of qualified brokers instead of a public campaign. And analytics that respect consent — because the people you’re marketing to value their privacy too.

What to do with your next listing

None of these trends demands an overhaul. Most brokers will get the greatest return from starting small and doing it properly:

  • At the shoot, capture vertical clips alongside the horizontal film.
  • At launch, give the aircraft its own shareable page, with the option to gate the details.
  • Behind the scenes, keep one source of truth for specifications and push it everywhere.
  • Before any of it, agree with the seller how visible they want to be.

The brokers who build these habits early won’t just look more modern. They’ll launch faster, keep their listings accurate and give sellers more control — which is exactly what wins the next listing.

The short version

Key takeaways

  1. Plan vertical video at the shoot: one idea per clip, key specs on screen.
  2. A dedicated microsite gives each aircraft one link, more control and better follow-up.
  3. Connected inventory keeps every channel consistent — and shows which channels work.
  4. Speed comes from removing the waiting, not from rushing the creative.
  5. Design for discretion from day one. For many sellers, it is part of the value.
