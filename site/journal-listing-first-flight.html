Your listing is the first flight

Long before a buyer climbs the airstair, they’ve already flown the aircraft — through its photographs, its film and its spec sheet. How to make that first flight count, and the checklist we use to launch a listing.

Every aircraft sale has two first flights. The second happens on a demo flight or during the pre-buy, with a pilot in the left seat and a buyer paying close attention. The first happens weeks earlier, on a phone screen, in a few seconds — and it decides whether there will ever be a second.

That first flight is the listing. It is the thumbnail that has to stop a scroll, the hero image that has to earn a click, the gallery and film that have to hold attention, and the spec sheet that has to survive the scrutiny of a broker, a chief pilot or an acquisition advisor. Long before anyone compares aircraft, they compare listings. Get yours right and every later conversation starts from confidence. Get it wrong and you spend the rest of the listing explaining.

First impressions arrive in sequence

It helps to stop thinking of a listing as a set of assets and start thinking of it as a route. A buyer moves through it in roughly the same order every time: a thumbnail among dozens of others, then the hero image, then the gallery, then — if they’re still interested — the film and the spec sheet, and finally the decision to get in touch.

Each step has one job: to earn the next. A thumbnail doesn’t need to tell the whole story; it needs to be the most compelling frame in a crowded grid. A spec sheet doesn’t need to be beautiful first; it needs to be complete, current and easy to verify. When every asset knows its job, the whole listing gets sharper.

Imagery: editorial, not inventory

There is a difference between documenting an aircraft and presenting it. Documentation proves the aircraft exists. Presentation makes someone want it. The best listings do both, in that order of prominence: editorial frames up front, thorough documentation right behind them.

Editorial imagery comes down to a few decisions made deliberately. Light: the soft hour around sunrise and sunset, or a clean, evenly lit hangar, flatters paint and reveals form. Angle: a low three-quarter view from the front gives an aircraft presence; a straight profile shows its proportions honestly. Inside, shoot from a seated eye line, because that is how the cabin will be experienced. Consistency: one color grade across the whole set, so the gallery reads as a single story rather than a collection of different days.

Editing matters too. Color correction, balanced exposure and a cleaner setting can turn a good frame into a great one. What editing should never do is change the aircraft. Wear stays. Scratches stay. The goal is a better photograph of the same airplane — because the buyer will eventually stand in front of the real one.

Long before anyone compares aircraft, they compare listings.

Film: let the buyer walk through

Photographs sell moments; film sells space. A well-made walk-through answers the questions still images can’t: how the cabin flows from the entry door to the aft lavatory, how much headroom there really is, how the light falls through the windows.

Shoot it the way a buyer would experience it: the approach across the ramp, the airstair, then the cabin zone by zone in boarding order, the galley, the lav, the cockpit and the baggage compartment. Keep the movement smooth and continuous so the viewer always knows where they are. Then cut it down. The full walk-through belongs on the listing and its microsite; shorter vertical cuts, captioned with the key specifications so they work with the sound off, belong on social and in the messages brokers send each other.

The spec sheet is the closer

Imagery opens the door. The spec sheet is what gets read line by line, forwarded and printed. It is the document every broker reads first, and it is often where a listing quietly loses credibility — through a missing date, a stale figure or a layout that buries what matters.

A strong spec sheet leads with the facts a buyer screens on: year, total time and cycles, engine program status, the most recent and next major inspections, seating, connectivity and avionics. Detail follows in clearly labeled sections. Use dates instead of adjectives, keep units consistent, and put an “as of” date on the document so everyone knows how current it is. Then make sure it works on a phone and on paper, because it will be read on both.

The listing launch checklist

Here is the sequence we work through. It isn’t exhaustive, but when every item is done, a listing goes live complete, consistent and ready to be judged.

Before the shoot

  • Confirm specifications, logbook status and any known items with the seller, in writing.
  • Agree the seller’s privacy needs: registration visible or masked, location shown or neutral.
  • Prepare the aircraft: exterior detailed, cabin staged and cleared of personal items.
  • Write a shot list covering hero frames, each cabin zone, the cockpit, details and working shots.

Production

  • Schedule exterior photography around the best light, not the most convenient hour.
  • Capture vertical and horizontal formats deliberately, rather than cropping one from the other later.
  • Film the walk-through in boarding order, plus short detail clips for social.

Launch week

  • Publish the same specifications, imagery and price position on every channel at once.
  • Send the spec sheet and film directly to the brokers and buyers most likely to act.
  • Release social cutdowns over the first days, each focused on a single feature.

After launch

  • Track where enquiries come from, and how qualified they are.
  • Report to the seller every week, in plain language.
  • If the listing goes quiet, refresh the imagery or positioning — don’t just repost it.

Launch once, launch right

A listing is never more visible than when it is new. That is the moment the market looks hardest, and the moment a half-finished gallery or a provisional spec sheet costs the most. Relaunching a tired listing is always harder than launching a complete one.

So treat the launch like the first flight it is: prepared, checked and flown in order. The buyer who sees it may be months away from a demo. But the decision to book one is being made right now.

The short version

Key takeaways

  1. Treat a listing as a route: every asset exists to earn the next step.
  2. Lead with editorial imagery, back it with thorough documentation, and never edit the aircraft itself.
  3. Film the cabin in boarding order, then cut vertical, captioned versions for social.
  4. Spec sheets win on dates, consistent units and an “as of” line — and must work on a phone and on paper.
  5. Launch complete. A listing is never more visible than when it’s new.
